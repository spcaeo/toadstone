The name

A toadstone is a gem that does not exist.

For about five hundred years, educated people across Europe believed that a jewel grew inside the head of an old toad. Pliny mentions it; so does Shakespeare, in the line about adversity wearing a precious jewel in its head. You were supposed to get one by laying a toad on a red cloth until it gave the stone up. They were set into rings and worn, and they had one job.

A toadstone was said to detect poison. Held near something harmful, it would grow warm, sweat, or change colour. You wore it so that you would know.

The stones were real objects. They were sold, inherited, listed in wills, and they turn up in museum collections today. They are the fossilised tooth plates of Lepidotes, a Jurassic fish — button-shaped, domed, faintly polished, and nothing whatever to do with toads. They never detected anything. People wore them for centuries, and felt safer, and were not.

That is why the name is on this tool.

The thing this project spent its first days learning

A crawler is a detector. You point it at a site and it tells you what is wrong. The entire value of it rests on one assumption: that when it says nothing is wrong, nothing is wrong.

On the day this fork got its own name, somebody measured that assumption for the first time. The project had 225 checks. 84 of them had ever been observed to fire. The rest passed their unit tests and sat inert on every real crawl — eight AMP checks that could not fire on any site because the markup they read was never kept; every PDF check, dead the moment JavaScript rendering was switched on; a raw-versus-rendered comparison that had been comparing a document against itself; a translation check that missed its commonest case. A failed PageSpeed run reported as a clean bill of performance. Three "checks" that were not checks at all, only sample data left in a test file.

None of that looked broken. Every report came back clean and confident, and clean was the wrong answer. The tool was a fossil fish tooth in a ring: worn in good faith, detecting nothing, and all the more dangerous for how reassuring it was.

Eleven of those faults were found by one method — build a page that should make a check fire, then watch it stay silent. A twelfth was in the measuring instrument itself, which gave each crawl two minutes and then reported whatever had happened by then as a pass.

So: Toadstone

The name is a reminder, pointed at ourselves.

Most tools in this field are named for what they do to a website — they crawl it, spider it, audit it, scan it. The verb is always something done to a site by something slightly predatory, and the reports read that way: a wall of findings, scored and ranked, handed over as a verdict.

But a detector that has never been shown to detect is just jewellery. The question that matters is not how many checks a tool advertises. It is how many of them have been watched to fire, on a real crawl, against a page built to be wrong — and what the tool does when it cannot answer, which must never be to go quiet and let silence read as health.

We count that number in public, in BUILD-LOG.md, including the parts that are stubbed, the parts that rest on someone else's API, and the one check proven with a model's answer supplied rather than asked for. When a check cannot honestly be reached, it says so rather than being quietly dropped from the total.

A toadstone that works would be worth wearing. That is the whole ambition.

Pronunciation. TOHD-stohn. As it looks.

Also known as: bufonite, from bufo, the toad genus — the name used when the fossils were finally identified for what they are.

Where Toadstone comes from

Toadstone is a fork of LibreCrawl, an open-source crawler written by Phiality and released under the MIT licence. The crawling engine, the multi-tenant model and the plugin system are theirs. We renamed the fork because it has grown its own opinions, not because we think we started it.

The MIT licence asks only that the original copyright notice travels with the software. It does, and we go further than the licence requires, because a single developer giving away work that replaces a £199-a-year product is the reason any of this exists.
